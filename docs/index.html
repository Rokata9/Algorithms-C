You can write C. You have not taken an algorithms class. That is enough.

You can know how far every place is without ranking the places in order.

Imagine a city map. You stand at one café. You want the travel time from that café to every other place. Not the route drawn on a map — just the numbers. For decades the standard computer method always handled the closest unfinished place next. That forces the computer to keep a giant “who is nearest?” list in order. In 2025 a team led from Tsinghua University showed you can skip that ranking and still get every number right. This page teaches that idea slowly.

2. The job

You are given:

  • A bunch of places (a café, a bridge, a station…).
  • One-way roads between some of them. Each road has a travel time that is zero or bigger. Never negative.
  • One start place. We will call it s because every textbook does. It is just a name.

You must fill an array with one number per place: the smallest total travel time from s to that place.

You do not have to list the places from nearest to farthest. You only need the numbers. That sentence is the whole plot of this page.

What “shortest” means here Not fewest roads. Smallest sum of travel times. A route with three short roads can beat a route with one long road.

4. Two numbers per place

While the algorithm runs, each place has a best guess so far. At the start:

  • Start place: guess = 0 (you are already there).
  • Everyone else: guess = “huge” (we have no route yet). In C you use a big sentinel, like INT_MAX or a double infinity.

Whenever you already have a guess for place u, a road from u to v with time w suggests a new guess for v:

new_guess_for_v = guess[u] + w

If that is cheaper than the guess you already had for v, replace it. People call this offering a road, or in textbooks, relaxing the road. Same thing: “maybe this road is a better deal.”

A guess becomes locked in when the algorithm decides it will never get cheaper. After that, the number is the answer for that place and we never change it. The exact test Dijkstra uses is in the next section — it is not “the guess looks right.”

Remember just this Best guess can only go down. Locked in never changes. A place can already have the true cheapest time and still be waiting. Guess being correct and place being locked in are two different events.

5. The standard method: always lock the nearest leftover place

This is Dijkstra’s method. Named after Edsger Dijkstra, 1950s. Almost every GPS-style textbook starts here.

There are only two kinds of step. They alternate. Locking only happens in the first kind.

PICK

Look at the waiting list. Take the place with the smallest guess. Lock that place in. This is the only moment a place becomes locked in. Then we start offering that place’s roads.

OFFER

Look at one road out of the place we just locked. If it gives a neighbour a cheaper guess, update that neighbour on the waiting list. Nobody is locked in during an offer. We do every remaining road of that place before the next pick.

The waiting list is a priority queue (in C, usually a binary heap): closest guess at the top. “Reordered” only means a guess got cheaper, so that place moves closer to the top. It does not mean we lock it.

The lock-in test, exactly A leftover place is locked in when it is at the top of the waiting list and we are in a PICK step. Why that is safe: every leftover place is at least as far as this one, and extra roads only add more time, so no unfinished route can beat this guess. Having the true number already is not the test. After we lock b, a’s guess becomes 3 (the real answer) while we are still offering b’s other roads. a stays gold until the next PICK.

    for each road u → v with time w:            // OFFER — no locking in this loop
        if guess[u] + w is cheaper than guess[v]:
            guess[v] = guess[u] + w
            if v is already on waiting:
                move v toward the top           // "reorder": cheaper guess, closer to top
            else:
                put v on waiting

      6. Why that waiting list is expensive

      Look at the loop. Every single place, we ask: “who on the waiting list is smallest right now?”

      That is the same kind of work as keeping a list of names in alphabetical order, or keeping high scores sorted. Each time you insert or pull the top item, you pay extra comparisons. Do that for every place, and the extra work grows faster than just looking at each place once.

      People call this extra cost “like sorting.” Not because the algorithm prints a ranked table, but because it is constantly maintaining order on that waiting list.

      Left: a leaderboard. Right: a table of numbers. Same information about times. The leaderboard is extra.

      The crack in the old method If you insist on always locking the globally nearest leftover place, you are building that leaderboard whether you wanted it or not. To go faster, you must sometimes lock a place that is not the closest leftover one. The numbers still have to come out right. The order you finish them can be a bit scrambled.

      That is all “breaking the sorting barrier” means. Not a physics barrier. A habit: we thought shortest-routes had to rank places. They don’t, if you only need the array.

      7. Another old method: everyone shouts, no waiting list

      There is a second classic method (Bellman–Ford). It does not keep a sorted waiting list at all.

      The rule: offer every road, then do it again, then again. After 1 round, every cheapest route that uses at most 1 road is correct. After 2 rounds, routes of at most 2 roads are correct. And so on.

      No ranking. The cost is: you look at every road, many times. If you repeat as many times as there are places, this is slower than Dijkstra. If you only repeat a few times, it is a cheap scout.

      Round 0

      Each Next is one shout-round: every place that already has a guess offers its roads.

      // A few shout-rounds. No sorted list anywhere.
guess[s] = 0
guess[everyone else] = HUGE
repeat a small number of times:
    for each road u → v with time w:
        if guess[u] + w is cheaper than guess[v]:
            guess[v] = guess[u] + w
      This looks like the “dumb” method. That is useful. Full shouting is slow. The 2025 algorithm only shouts a handful of times, on purpose, as a probe: “after two shouts, which places still have a crowd hanging off them?” Those crowded places are the busy intersections. Coming next.

        9. After those offer-rounds: which starts we keep

        Same wide visual — the big map, ownership trees, and keep/drop pictures live there.

        The last section skipped PICK and only OFFERed. Next: after those OFFERs, which locked starts do we still use as sources? The six-place map is the wrong demo for that (from s alone you already reach a crowd). This map makes the choice visible.

        Three starts, already locked, guess 0: x, y, z. Each begins a chain. Every road has time 1, so a guess is just “how many roads from a start.”

        Green = current starts (already locked). Top chain is long, middle is medium, bottom is short. We will do k = 3 offer-rounds from x, y, and z together — no PICK.

        Each time a guess improves, record parent[neighbour] = the place we offered from, same as you can do in Dijkstra. After the offer-rounds, follow parent until you hit a start. That start owns this place. Count how many places each start owns (including itself).

        A start is a hub if it owns at least k places. We keep hubs as sources for the rest of the algorithm. We drop the other starts: their short chains already got the right guess in those k rounds, so we do not need them as sources any more.

        There is an early exit: if the number of places we touched is already bigger than k × (number of starts), the start list is already small compared with the work. Then we keep every start and skip the counting. On the six-place map that exit fires. On this map it does not, so you will see the counting.

          FIND-HUBS(time_limit, starts):
    touched = copy of starts
    layer = starts
    repeat k times:
        next_layer = empty
        for each road out of a place in layer:
            offer that road
            if the neighbour's new guess is still under time_limit:
                add neighbour to next_layer and to touched
                parent[neighbour] = the place we offered from
        if size of touched > k * size of starts:
            return all starts as hubs     // early exit
        layer = next_layer
    for each start, count how many touched places follow parent[] back to it
    hubs = starts whose count is at least k
    return hubs
          In C touched is an array of ids plus a boolean “already listed.” parent[] is the same array you can keep in Dijkstra when a guess improves. Count “owned” by walking from every touched place up parent until you hit a start, and add 1 to that start’s counter. Or walk the tree downward. Same numbers.

          10. Why we recurse, and why we stop early

          After you have hubs, you still should not sort them all. You put them on a pile (next section) and repeat:

          1. Grab a group of the closest hubs — a handful, not one.
          2. Solve the same kind of job for that group, with a tighter time limit (“only finish people who are about this close”).
          3. That smaller job is the same function calling itself on a smaller piece. That is all “recursion” means here. A loop with a stack would do the same.
          4. When the smaller job returns, offer roads out of the places it locked in. Newly improved neighbours go back on the pile.
          5. If the pile is empty, you finished everything under the original time limit. If you have already locked in a lot of places, stop anyway and tell the caller “I did the closest chunk; here is a new, smaller time limit.” The caller will grab another group. That early stop is not a failure. It keeps each job a manageable size.
          Smaller job, closer places Each recursive call only cares about a nearer slice of remaining times. Sibling groups do not overlap. Earlier groups are the closer places. That is how the method still moves outward from the start, just not one-by-one.

          11. The pile is not a high-score table

          A normal heap is built to answer one question well: “what is the single smallest item?” Dijkstra asks that question constantly. We ask a weaker question:

          “Give me about M of the smallest items, and a cutoff number that separates them from whatever is left.”

          Weaker questions can be cheaper. You do not keep every item in total order. You keep them in blocks of about M items. Blocks themselves are in order (this block is closer than that block). Inside a block, messy is fine.

          The pile also has a cheap “dump these on the front” operation, used when you already know a batch of places is closer than everything currently on the pile. You do not search for a slot. You stick a new block at the front.

          You sort blocks, not every individual place. That is the trick.

          When a block grows past M items, split it in half around the middle value. For a first C version, sorting that one block is fine. The paper uses a fancier linear-time median. Same idea.

          In C this is the fiddly piece Two linked lists of blocks. Each block is a small array of {place, guess}. Remember where each place currently sits so a better guess can replace the old pair. Get “insert” and “grab a group” working with tests that have nothing to do with maps. Then add “dump on the front.”

          12. The whole loop, as a flowchart

          The paper names this job “bounded multi-source shortest path.” Forget the name. In English it is:

          The job one function call does You get some already-locked-in start places, and a time limit. Finish every place whose cheapest route goes through those starts and whose cheapest time is under the limit. If that is too much work, finish only the closest chunk and return a tighter limit. The very first call is: one start s, time limit “none.” That one call is the original problem.

          14. The recipe you can implement

          English first. Then the same steps with the names the paper uses, so you can match a PDF later.

          FINISH(how_deep, time_limit, starts):
    if how_deep is 0:                       // smallest kind of job
        walk like Dijkstra from the one start
        stop after k+1 places, and never go past time_limit
        return the places we locked in

    hubs, touched = FIND-HUBS(time_limit, starts)
    put each hub on an empty pile, with its current guess
    locked = empty

    while the pile is not empty and locked is not already huge:
        group, tighter_limit = grab about M closest from the pile
        newly = FINISH(how_deep - 1, tighter_limit, group)
        add newly to locked
        for each road out of a newly locked place:
            offer the road
            if the neighbour improved:
                if they still sit between tighter_limit and time_limit:
                    insert them on the pile
                if they are closer than tighter_limit:
                    remember them to dump on the front of the pile
        dump those closer ones on the front
        also dump any group members that were not finished

    add anyone in touched whose guess is still under the limit we reached
    return locked

          Top-level call, once:

          guess[s] = 0
guess[everyone else] = HUGE
FINISH(how_deep = a small integer, time_limit = none, starts = {s})
          When you offer a road, use ≤ not < If the new guess is equal to the old guess, still treat the road as used. The recursive layers reuse roads. If you write if (new < old) in C, you will drop places. Break leftover ties by place id so the parent pointers stay a tree.
          Optional: the two knobs, and the paper’s time claim

          There are two knobs. k is how many shouts. t controls how big a group you grab and how many times you recurse. The paper sets them from the number of places so the shouting work and the pile work stay balanced. Copy the formulas when you code. You do not need to invent them:

          k = floor( (log2 of n) ^ (1/3) )     // still 2–3 on huge maps
t = floor( (log2 of n) ^ (2/3) )
how_deep = ceil( (log2 of n) / t )    // a handful of recursion levels
group size M at a given depth uses t as well

          On a map with about as many roads as places, Dijkstra’s extra waiting-list work grows like sorting all the places. The new extra work grows more slowly. That is the headline result. It is a paper-faster, not a “your phone will feel it” faster. Next section.

          15. Will this make your GPS faster?

          No. Not on maps you can actually store. People have implemented this faithfully and timed it against ordinary Dijkstra with a binary heap. Dijkstra won, by several times, on road maps with tens of millions of places. The new method does more bookkeeping per place. The “slower growing extra work” only wins if the map is so huge that the growth rate beats that extra bookkeeping. Published estimates say that may be beyond any graph that fits in memory.

          So why bother?

          • You wanted to understand a real 2025 result well enough to write it.
          • It is a clean lesson: the job you think you are solving (rank every place) might be stricter than the job you were asked (fill an array).
          • On dense maps (almost every place connected to every other) Dijkstra was already fine. The win is about maps that look like roads: not many roads per place.

          17. Mistakes that actually break a C port

          • Offering a road only when the new guess is strictly smaller. Use ≤.
          • Sorting the whole boundary “just while I get it working.” Then you have Dijkstra plus overhead, and you will think the paper is wrong.
          • Testing hub-finding on 20 places with the real formula for k. On small n, k becomes 1 and the interesting branch barely fires. Force k = 2 while you learn.
          • One shared heap across recursive calls. Each tiny walk owns a heap. Each FINISH call owns a pile. The only globals are guess[] and parent[].
          • Treating “we stopped early and returned a tighter limit” as a bug. That is how a parent splits work into groups.

          18. Check yourself

          One best answer each. Click before you peek at the note underneath.

          1. Dijkstra always locks in the leftover place with the smallest guess. Why can a different method still be correct?

          If the output is an array of numbers, the order you lock places in does not have to be the order of those numbers. You still must not lock a place in too early — the new method is careful about that — but it does not keep a full leaderboard.

          2. A place is “locked in” when…

          Locked in = finished. Boundary places are not automatically finished.

          3. Why shout only a few times when looking for hubs?

          Negative times are not allowed in this algorithm at all. Shouting is a probe, not a full solve.

          4. Grabbing from the pile returns…

          That handful is the start set of the next smaller job. Asking for a handful is cheaper than keeping a total ranking.

          5. You implement this carefully and time it against heap-Dijkstra on a 100,000-place road map. Dijkstra is faster. Did you fail?

          The 2025 algorithm does not use randomness. It is slower in practice on tested sizes. That is expected.

          6. The call that solves the original job (one start, every place) is…

          Everything else is a helper. That one top call is the whole problem.
